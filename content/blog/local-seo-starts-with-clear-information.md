Local SEO helps people find and choose a business that can serve them where they are. For a small business, the practical work starts with accurate business information, pages that answer buying questions, and evidence that makes the company’s experience tangible. A city name helps establish the location. The rest of the page needs to earn the visit.

This guide connects three kinds of work: accurate business information, useful website pages, and original evidence. It is for service businesses, local shops, and small teams deciding where to spend limited attention. You’ll find a page-planning test, a research framework, and a way to measure progress without promising a map position.

## Separate the map listing from the website

Your Google Business Profile and your website are connected, but they are different surfaces. A profile can present practical business details in Google’s local results. Your website gives you room to explain services, show work, and answer questions in depth.

Google says local results are mainly shaped by relevance, distance, and prominence or popularity. Accurate information can help it understand your business; it does not allow you to choose a searcher’s distance from you or guarantee a ranking. [Google’s local ranking guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091) is the source for those factors.

A useful plan gives each surface a job. Put current operating information on the profile. Make the linked website page continue the same story. If the profile advertises a service, the visitor should be able to find a clear explanation of that service after clicking through.

## Establish a dependable business record

Create a short internal record of the facts you want to keep accurate: public business name, contact details, website, opening or appointment arrangements, core services, and actual service area. Identify who can approve changes and who updates each location where the information appears.

Then review the places a customer is likely to encounter it. Prioritize the website, the business profile, and genuinely relevant listings rather than trying to create an entry on every directory you can find.

| Information | Question to resolve | Common source of confusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name | What name does the business use with customers? | Old and new names appear without explanation |
| Contact | Which channel is monitored? | A published number or inbox nobody checks |
| Hours | When can people visit or expect the service to operate? | General hours mistaken for appointment availability |
| Service area | Where can the business actually perform the work? | Broad city lists that exceed operating capacity |
| Destination page | Where should someone go from the profile? | A generic page that hides the advertised service |

This is also a maintenance task. A correct profile can become inaccurate after a move, a service change, or a staff transition. Put the review alongside those business changes rather than treating it as a one-time SEO exercise.

### Describe the location honestly

A storefront, a business that travels to customers, and an online-only business are not interchangeable. Google’s eligibility and address rules matter. Its guidelines say businesses generally need in-person customer contact to qualify; service-area businesses that do not serve customers at their address should hide that address. Check the [business representation guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) against your actual setup before creating or editing a profile.

Do not invent a location to reach another market. On the website, explain where you work in terms a customer can use. If travel or scheduling affects whether you can accept a job, make that part of the conversation. Precision is more helpful than pretending the business is everywhere.

### Make reviews part of the customer handoff

Choose a consistent moment to invite customers to describe their experience, such as after a completed job or purchase. Make the invitation easy to act on and neutral about what the customer should say. Google’s [review guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122) explains how to share a review link or QR code. Its [contribution policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) prohibits incentives and selectively soliciting positive reviews.

A useful operating routine is simple: one person owns the invitation, another step records whether it was sent, and someone monitors replies. When responding publicly, address the experience without disclosing private project details. Reviews also provide editorial clues. If customers repeatedly praise how you explained their options, that may be a strength your service page has barely mentioned.

## Build service pages around different decisions

The strongest starting point is often the service someone needs, not the place name. Installation, repair, and refinishing may lead to different customer questions even when they are available in the same area.

For each important service, record:

- The situation that brings someone to the page.
- The work you offer and the limits of that offer.
- The questions people ask before making contact.
- Relevant projects, photographs, or explanations you can substantiate.
- The information you need for the next conversation.

Turn that record into a page that can stand on its own. Avoid repeating the same introduction across every service. Give the visitor enough detail to recognize whether they are in the right place, then link to a related option when it may fit better.

**Hypothetical example:** a homeowner wants to extend hardwood into an adjoining room. A useful page can explain what information is needed to assess the existing floor and the new space. “Best flooring in your city” leaves the actual problem unanswered.

### Match the question to the right page

Write down the words people use in calls, inquiries, and consultations. Group them by the decision involved before deciding how many pages to create. Search wording varies; several phrases may belong on the same page.

| Customer question | Likely page | What earns its place there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Can you refinish my floor?” | Refinishing service page | Suitable situations, assessment process, limitations, and inquiry requirements |
| “Do you work in my area?” | Service-area section or page | Actual coverage and any scheduling or travel conditions |
| “Should I repair or replace it?” | Comparison guide linked from both services | Decision criteria, uncertainty, and when an assessment is needed |
| “Have you handled something like this?” | Relevant project story | Starting condition, work performed, photographs, and explained decisions |
| “What happens after I contact you?” | Service page and inquiry confirmation | Who responds, what information helps, and the actual next step |

This table is a planning example, not a prescription for five new pages. If your existing page answers the question well, improve its navigation or headings before creating another destination. Our [website planning guide](/blog/what-your-business-website-needs/) explains how to turn those decisions into a manageable page plan.

## Decide whether a location page has a reason to exist

A location page is useful when the place changes what the visitor needs to know and you have evidence or operational detail to explain that difference. It is weak when it simply swaps a city name into a shared template.

Use this editorial test before creating one:

| Question | Evidence that supports a page | Reason to pause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do we serve this place? | A defined operating area and ability to fulfill inquiries | An aspiration to serve it someday |
| Is there a distinct information need? | Relevant access, process, service, or project detail | The same explanation already answers the question |
| Can we contribute something specific? | Approved work examples or useful local knowledge | A stock photo and generic praise for the area |
| Is there a useful next step? | A relevant service or inquiry path | A page that only sends everyone to another generic page |

This is a writing and planning test, not a Google scoring formula. It helps you decide whether another page deserves the reader’s time. Google’s [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) identify doorway abuse as pages created to target similar queries and funnel users onward. Repetition is not a substitute for an answer.

You can often improve the existing service page instead. A clearly written service-area section may resolve the visitor’s question without introducing ten pages you now need to maintain.

### A completed page brief: improving one service page

Consider a hypothetical flooring contractor receiving inquiries about extending an existing floor. This example is an editorial exercise, not a description of a client’s results.

**Reader’s decision:** “Can this company help connect the flooring in two rooms, and what does it need to assess the job?”

**Page decision:** improve the existing installation page. The question concerns a service already offered across the operating area; a new page for every nearby town would not resolve it better.

**Proposed section heading:** “Extending hardwood into another room.”

**Content to commission:** ask the installer what they inspect before recommending an approach, what may limit a close match, and which photographs help prepare for a visit. Have the installer review the explanation before publication. Avoid promising an invisible match that has not been assessed.

**Evidence to obtain:** permission to use photographs from a relevant completed project, with captions identifying the existing area, the new work, and the decision each image illustrates. If a project is still underway, label its status.

**Next step:** an inquiry asking for the town, a short description of the existing floor and planned work, and the preferred contact method. Only request information the team will use.

**Definition of done:** the explanation is approved, images have permission and useful captions, the service area is clear, the inquiry works on a phone, and a named person is responsible for following up.

**What to review afterward:** whether incoming inquiries are a better fit and whether customers still need the same explanation before an assessment. Search visibility is another observation, not the sole definition of a successful page.

The useful distinction is the decision to improve one existing page. Content planning should sometimes result in fewer URLs and a stronger answer.

## Turn real work into evidence

A project can show how you approach a problem in a way a claim about “quality service” cannot. Document the starting situation, your role, the decisions involved, the work delivered, and what you can actually say about the result.

Ask permission before sharing identifiable customer details or photographs. Remove unnecessary personal information. A useful case study does not need the customer’s home address, private correspondence, or internal business records.

Separate three kinds of statements:

- **Intent:** what the project was designed to accomplish.
- **Deliverable:** what was created or changed.
- **Outcome:** what was observed, with enough context to interpret it.

A completed page is a deliverable. An increase in inquiries is an outcome that needs a source, a period, and a definition. Neither automatically establishes what caused a broader business result.

## A worked example: original research for TGB Flooring

Our [TGB Flooring project](/work/tgb-flooring/) included a comparison of **117 Highlands Ranch home listings**, with **72 identifying hardwood and 45 identifying laminate**. We developed an HTML resource with summary statistics, a comparison table, methodology, limitations, homeowner questions, and structured data describing the article and dataset.

The question connected the company’s work with information homeowners might want to explore. It also required restraint. Differences in asking prices within that sample could not establish what installing hardwood would add to a particular home’s value.

Why not? A home’s listed price can vary with many characteristics besides the flooring label. Location, size, condition, and other differences may affect the comparison. A listing is also not a completed sale. These are limitations a reader needs in order to interpret the observation.

The contribution of this project was a specific local resource with visible boundaries. We do not have evidence here to claim it generated a particular number of leads, links, or rankings. You can explain the quality of the work without adding an outcome you have not measured.

### What another business can borrow from this approach

Borrow the structure of the inquiry, not the subject or the numbers. A repair business might already have a useful set of recurring customer questions. A retailer might be able to explain a product-selection problem with documented examples. Start with information you can lawfully use and accurately describe.

A small, well-defined question is easier to investigate than a sweeping claim about an entire market. “What information is missing from the inquiries we receive?” could lead to a better service page. It does not require pretending you have conducted an industry-wide survey.

## Plan a small research project before collecting data

Write the method before you know the result. That makes it easier to notice when the project has drifted toward finding a convenient marketing claim.

### 1. Define the question and the unit

What exactly are you studying: a listing, an inquiry, a project, or a product? Define what counts as one record. Repeated versions of the same record can distort a small sample if they are treated as separate observations.

### 2. Set the inclusion rules

Specify the source, period, geography if relevant, and conditions for including or excluding a record. If a key field is missing, decide how you will handle it and disclose that choice. Do not silently turn an unknown value into a negative result.

### 3. Keep an audit trail

Record the source, collection date, and any changes you make to the data. Preserve enough information to check the analysis, while keeping private information out of the published version. If you do not have permission to reproduce source material, describe your method without publishing the protected material.

### 4. Choose the comparison before the headline

Decide what you can reasonably summarize. Counts may be sufficient. If you compare groups, show their sizes and describe meaningful differences you could not account for. A precise-looking percentage can still be misleading when the underlying sample is small or selective.

### 5. Publish the limits beside the finding

Readers should not have to discover a major qualification at the bottom of the page. Put the scope and key limitation next to the result. Show the method in enough detail for someone to understand what was done, even when you cannot share the raw data.

## Give the resource a place in the website

An original article should connect naturally to the service it informs. Link from the relevant service page when the resource answers a likely question. In the article, explain the relationship to the service without turning every section into a sales pitch.

Use a descriptive title, headings that explain the structure, and a short introduction stating what the reader will learn. Tables need labels and surrounding interpretation. A chart should show where its data comes from and what the numbers represent. If an image carries essential information, include that information in text too.

Treat the resource as something to maintain. Record when the data was collected and when the page was materially updated. If the findings become outdated, explain that or revise the analysis; changing the date alone does not make the underlying work current.

### Check that people and search engines can reach it

Before commissioning more writing, verify the new resource is actually available:

1. Open its public URL while signed out. Confirm it loads the intended page, and test the contact or booking path on a phone.
2. Follow a link to it from a relevant page. Google’s [link guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/links-crawlable) recommends ordinary links with destination URLs and descriptive text. A page that exists only in an internal document is easy to overlook.
3. Use [Search Console’s URL Inspection tool](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9012289) to examine Google’s indexed version and, separately, test the live page. Ask your developer to investigate an unintended indexing block or a different canonical page. A successful live test does not guarantee indexing.
4. Check that the title, main heading, and introduction describe the same subject. Open the article’s sources and internal links; make sure they support the statements beside them.
5. Assign the next maintenance action: verify coverage when operations change, update project status when work finishes, or revisit findings when the data becomes stale.

These checks establish a usable, discoverable resource. They are not a ranking forecast.

## Measure the parts you can observe

Separate visibility, use, and business relevance. Search impressions can tell you that a page appeared. Clicks can tell you that someone chose it. Neither alone tells you whether the person needed your service or whether the page changed their decision.

For an inquiry-driven business, review the questions and locations in actual inquiries alongside available search information. Look for patterns: the wrong service, the wrong area, repeated confusion, or questions the page already ought to answer.

Keep a dated change log. Record what you changed and what else happened around that time, such as a promotion or seasonal shift. Observing improvement after an edit is useful, but it does not by itself isolate the edit as the cause. The [marketing measurement guide](/blog/marketing-measures-that-matter/) expands on this distinction.

### Diagnose the pattern before choosing the next edit

In Search Console, filter to the page you changed and examine the queries, impressions, and clicks over a stated period. Compare periods of equal length and note seasonal differences. Query reporting is incomplete—Google omits some queries for privacy—so it is one source of evidence, not a full record of demand. See [Google’s explanation of query reporting](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/17011259).

Use observations to choose an investigation:

| Observation | What to investigate next | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Page is not indexed | URL Inspection, accidental blocks, duplicate/canonical handling, internal links | That the topic has no demand |
| Page appears for unrelated questions | Whether the title, page scope, and service explanation match the intended reader | That repeating a target keyword will fix it |
| Relevant visits arrive, but inquiries do not | Offer clarity, proof, contact usability, and whether visitors are ready to act | That all visitors were potential buyers |
| Inquiries repeatedly concern an unavailable service or area | Coverage wording, exclusions, profile details, and the referral path | That more traffic is the immediate priority |
| Inquiries fit, but follow-up is slow | Ownership, response process, and operational capacity | That another article will solve the bottleneck |

Do not redesign the plan around one inquiry or a brief fluctuation. For a business with very few visits, direct customer questions and a functional inquiry process may be more actionable than unstable percentages.

## Where to begin with limited time

Use a sequence with clear completion criteria. The following is an implementation order, not a promise about when search results will change.

| Work block | Deliverable | Move on when… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Correct the basics | One approved business record and a working inquiry path | Public details match actual operations and a test inquiry reaches the right person |
| Improve the priority service | One page answering the main buying questions | A knowledgeable team member has checked the explanation, evidence, and next step |
| Document real experience | One approved project story or carefully scoped resource | Claims can be traced to evidence and limitations appear beside findings |
| Connect and review | Relevant internal links and a dated review note | You can explain what changed, what you observed, and which question to investigate next |

Start with the service you can deliver well and want more suitable inquiries for. If the first block reveals a broken form, fix that before researching an ambitious report. If prospective customers already find you but misunderstand the offer, improve that explanation before expanding geographic coverage.

### Do I need original research to compete locally?

No. Accurate information, helpful service pages, credible project evidence, and attentive follow-up are worthwhile on their own. Research is a good investment when there is a real unanswered question and you have the information and capacity to investigate it. A modest case study can be more useful than a survey your team cannot support.

### Do I need to mention every city I serve?

You need to make the actual service area understandable. That may be a concise list or a clear explanation on a service page. It does not automatically require a separate article or landing page for every place.

### Does original research guarantee links or rankings?

No. It gives you an opportunity to contribute something specific and useful. Whether others discover, cite, or act on it depends on the work, the audience, and circumstances beyond the article itself.

### What if I have no data yet?

Begin with questions. Record what prospective customers need to understand and improve the page that should answer them. You can build a disciplined evidence base over time without presenting early observations as a formal study.
